## The problem

Discovery on a streaming service is passive. The system learns taste from what you finish watching, and finishing is a 30-minute commitment—so the signal is expensive, sparse, and late. You scroll, you hover, you sometimes press play—and what the system actually learns from is the finish, which is the expensive part. I worked on the platforms that run Disney+ and Hulu, where that gap is structural.

Swipe Watch is the gap made concrete: deal real titles as cards and let taste cost a gesture instead of a viewing session. Swipe left to dismiss, right for interested, up to flag a title for later. Sessions deal ten cards at a time from a hand-curated pool of 106 Disney+ and Hulu titles—series, films, specials—viewed through four discovery modes: Disney Vault, Streaming Originals, Nature & Discovery, and New & Trending. The modes are not separate lists; they are read-time filters over one flat pool, and the last is just a year predicate. Session state lives on the device and there is no application backend—the swipe events go to analytics, the one thing that leaves the phone.

## The hypothesis, stated late

Everything in this section is reconstruction, and that needs saying plainly. The repository records no hypothesis, no success criteria, and no rejected alternatives—not in its tree, not in its six specs, not anywhere in its 97 issues and pull requests. The specs exist, but all six arrived in a single template-conformance commit 31 days after the core build, and each one restates behavior that had already shipped; a spec written a month after the code is the tree describing itself, not a design the tree was built to. So what follows is the bet as I can state it now, in retrospect—and a criterion written after the evidence, which the evidence happens to meet, is not a criterion the experiment was run against. Nothing on this page grades the prototype against these criteria and calls the result a pass.

The primary hypothesis: lower-cost preference actions generate denser intent signal than completed viewing history—many cheap reactions beat one expensive one. The target user is a streaming subscriber who browses more than they commit. The expected behavior: dealt ten cards, they work the deck to the bottom and reach for the save gesture when a title genuinely tempts them. Success would have looked like full sessions and return visits; failure like stalled decks and one-and-done curiosity. One thing that criterion cannot be is unprompted, and the reason is visible further down this page: the app opens with a walkthrough that names all three gestures, so nothing in the record distinguishes a gesture someone discovered from one the overlay taught them. Discoverability was unobservable by construction, and what is left is a criterion about post-onboarding behavior only. The instrument—analytics from the first commit, five actions then and six after the economy shipped—could have counted sessions and swipes. It did capture which titles were flagged, since the save event carries the card's title. What no custom action captured is time on a card or whether anything was kept rather than merely flagged. Return behaviour is the exception and cuts the other way: GA4 records session starts and first visits automatically, so an export could show who came back even though nothing was wired to ask. So these reconstructed criteria were partly measurable and never measured.

A secondary hypothesis is scored separately below, because the two fail differently. The coin and unlock commits landed three days after the first—that much the repository dates—while the hypothesis attached to them, that coins and unlocks would address session completion and return motivation, is reconstructed here on the same terms as the first. That claim is a before-and-after; the swipe claim is a comparison. Neither borrows the other's support.

These are the conditions the record shows the experiment ran under.

## The decisions

A prototype's fidelity should match the narrowest question it can answer, and the narrow question here was feel: does swiping through a streaming catalog feel like expressing taste? Feel is answerable at prototype fidelity—it needs real titles, real art, and card physics, and it needs nothing else. Two harder questions were priced out and stayed out: whether the swipe signal actually beats viewing history, which needs a comparator this system does not contain, and whether anything here retains users, which needs a baseline nobody set up and nobody has since gone looking for. The core build is thirteen commits across three calendar days—a Tuesday, then a contiguous Friday-into-Saturday stretch—inside a five-day window. Three days is a count of the commit record, not a measure of effort: the first commit already ships its script tag at version 1.3, and whatever versions 1.0 through 1.2 were, the repository cannot date them.

A dedicated pass on the second evening handled the part interaction prototypes usually skip: a gesture demo animation, an idle-card pulse, continuous swipe-overlay feedback, a progress bar synced to the card exit, a post-swipe toast. Whether any of it made the first session self-explanatory is not something the record can say.

Capturing that card at a real phone width turned up a defect the stylesheet reads as correct. The synopsis is meant to clamp at two lines with an ellipsis, and a `flex: 1` on the same element resizes the box against the rest of the card; the two rules disagree. Sampling forty cards on the live site in late August 2026, the description box landed anywhere between two and 3.4 lines: on some cards the clamp's ellipsis is followed by a third line rendered below it, so the ellipsis announces a truncation that did not happen, and on three of the forty the synopsis is cut with no ellipsis and no affordance at all. No description in the catalog contains an ellipsis—all 106 were checked, not just the sampled forty—so every ellipsis on screen is the clamp's. It is a small bug, a fix for it is queued, and it is a fair illustration of what a three-day prototype defers: the interaction was checked by hand and by demo, never by measurement, and this is the kind of thing measurement finds. The one mobile bug that did get noticed at the time—poster art cropping and a badge clipping on small viewports—closed in a 21-line fix that April.

## What the record shows

Start with what the record is. The prototype was instrumented from its first commit—Google Analytics, five actions to begin with: like, dislike, super-like, onboarding completion, restart, joined by unlock when the economy shipped three days later—and the instrument is still live: fetched in late August 2026, the deployed document and script are byte-identical to the audited commit—those two files were diffed, not the whole hosted tree—and every swipe still fires an event. What no record anywhere contains is a read. Not one figure from that property was ever published—in the repository, its specs, or any of its 97 issues and pull requests—and this page will not be the first to invent one. The honest posture has three parts, and the middle one has a boundary worth keeping: the data exists; no figure from it has ever been published in anything this audit can search, and no read path to the property was available here; and no number from it appears on this page. Whether someone once opened the dashboard and looked is not something the repository can answer either way.

Even read, the data could not settle either hypothesis, and for different reasons—which is why this page keeps them apart. The swipe hypothesis is comparative: a swipe is a denser preference signal than viewing history. The comparator is not in the system—this prototype observes no viewing history of any kind—so the analytics could at most report how many swipes happened, never that they beat the alternative. The coin hypothesis is a before-and-after, and the before was never captured. The economy shipped three days and ten hours after the first commit, and no flag, variant, or cohort was ever set up. That is not the same as the two periods being indistinguishable: event timestamps and a known deployment boundary would separate pre-coin traffic from post-coin traffic in any export. What is unknown is whether that window holds any traffic at all, because nobody has queried it. A pre-coin window did exist in production: the initial commit carries both the analytics snippet and a Firebase hosting deploy cache, which the CLI only writes when a deploy has run, so the app was live and instrumented for those three days. Whether anyone used it in that window is unknown, because no figure from the property was ever published and no read of it is recoverable here. The claim is not merely unproven, then. The one window that could have grounded it was live and instrumented, and no examination of it survives in any published or recoverable record.

The one piece of qualitative evidence is a demo. I demoed Swipe Watch to Disney's EVP of Product, and a demo is what the record contains: no artifact in the repository or its 97 issues and pull requests mentions it, and the résumé line that repeats it traces to this same single source rather than corroborating it. Its ceiling as evidence is worth stating exactly—one demo, one room, one stakeholder, of a prototype whose own copy simulates the personalization it does not perform. How the room received it is not recorded anywhere, so the reception is unobservable and no reaction is claimed here. What a demo can be at its best is an occasion for qualitative stakeholder feedback about the idea: useful for deciding whether the question deserves a real test, structurally incapable of being that test. It does not confirm the user hypothesis, and this page does not enlist it to.

The save gesture deserves the same exactness. Two controls in the shipped UI promise a watchlist—swipe-up is labeled "Add to main watchlist," swipe-right "Add to training watchlist"—and no watchlist exists. The app persists exactly three pieces of local state—seen titles, the coin bank, an onboarding flag—and none of them holds a saved title; the save counter is in-memory and zeroed when the next session starts. What the gesture actually does is fire a save-for-later event to analytics, and that event carries the card's title. So the two boundaries are different and worth separating: the device forgets which titles were flagged, and the telemetry does not. A GA4 export would name them. That is a real signal, and it is the strongest claim the code supports. An earlier revision of this page said "building a watchlist"; that claim is withdrawn.

The only behavioral numbers this page can stand behind are about the page, not the app, and they are small. Over the 74 days this site's own analytics cover—June 14 through August 26, 2026, a window that opens fifteen weeks after the prototype shipped—this page recorded 18 pageviews across 14 sessions from 13 people, and exactly three clicks left it for the prototype: two to the live app, one to the repository. Those counts are unfiltered—my own visits are inside them, and bot traffic cannot be excluded—so they loosely bound the audience of this write-up and say nothing about how anyone used the app. Even that small number nearly came out wrong: the outbound-click events carry a project-attribution property that is null on every one of them, so the obvious query returns zero clicks rather than three, and only the page path recovers the truth. An instrument answering confidently in the wrong units is this project's whole evidence problem in miniature.

## Not a recommendation engine

Swipe Watch is not a recommendation engine, and the boundary is mechanical rather than rhetorical. There is no ML backend, no collaborative filtering, no personalization. The app makes no network calls at all beyond loading poster art and firing the analytics beacon; the "recommendation" logic is a mode filter, a seen-title exclusion, a shuffle, and a slice of ten. The preference signals are write-only—nothing in the app ever reads them back—so personalization is not merely absent but impossible with the current data model.

The shipped product says otherwise, and the gap is recorded in the decision ledger above rather than smoothed over here. The live app tells its user "Learning your taste…" after a swipe and "Based on your swipes, we've refined your recommendations" at the end of a session, and nothing behind those strings exists. That was the cost of demoing the loop whole, and it is the single biggest reason the qualitative evidence is capped where it is: a prototype that simulates the payoff it is testing for collects reactions to the idea, not to the thing.

## The operating model

The build predates the operating model the rest of this portfolio runs on. The core thirteen commits were direct pushes—not one of them carries a pull-request marker; the repository's first pull request, swipewatch#1, was merged 21 days after the initial commit, and it is the review policy itself. From there the prototype was brought under the same agent-run process as everything else. Template conformance added tests, lint, and the six retrospective specs. Automated review earned its keep at least once: nothing in the hand-entered catalog enforces uniqueness, and the one duplicate tile that shipped in the pool's final expansion was caught by CodeRabbit in review of swipewatch#20—not by the data model, and not by me—and removed in swipewatch#21. The one post-build behavioral bug ran issue-to-PR, swipewatch#29 to swipewatch#30, and closed in 21 lines. Ninety-seven issues and pull requests now stand against thirteen core commits, which is the honest shape of a prototype: the building was fast and unreviewed, and the process arrived with the maintenance.

## The bar for building more

What would justify building the back half—actual personalization? Not a bigger demo, and not this record. Three things, in order. First, the read with no published or recoverable result: pull the analytics property and bound how much the prototype was actually used, because six months of telemetry that has produced no recorded finding is a question the experiment has already half-answered by silence. Second, a save gesture with consequences: the telemetry already names every title anyone flagged, so what is missing is not the measurement but the product—a watchlist the user can actually see, which is what would make the gesture mean something to the person performing it rather than only to an export. Third, the comparator the current system lacks: some measure of the same users' viewing-history signal to put beside their swipe signal, because "denser than viewing history" is unfalsifiable inside a system that observes no viewing. Until those exist, the swipe stays what the record shows it to be—an interaction that was built, shipped, instrumented, and demoed, whose central claim remains precisely as open as the day it was made.
