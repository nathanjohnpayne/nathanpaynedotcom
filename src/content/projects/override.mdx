## The problem

I invest in Broadway productions. The first position I tracked arrived as three documents: a term sheet PDF, a cap-table spreadsheet, a subscription agreement by email. Three months later the cap table and the term sheet disagreed.

Nobody had done anything wrong. The documents were snapshots, and a snapshot stops being true the moment anything changes—which on a production being capitalized is constantly. A Broadway deal is a working model circulated as dead paper: every change obligates somebody to reissue all of it and hope the versions converge on the way back.

Override is the model instead of the paper—one maintained structure a producer runs the show's money on and shares with backers, built for the producer's side of the table by someone sitting on the investor's.

## The name

The override, as I learned the word, is the producer's percentage taken off the top: the share that comes out before investors see a distribution. Naming the product after a line in the waterfall is the whole positioning—opaque to anyone outside the industry, immediately legible to any producer who has ever negotiated one. That is my reading rather than a provable fact, and it is also the promise. A tool called Override had better know where the money goes.

## What it computes

Broadway money moves in a fixed order, and the order is the product. Each week, gross box office less credit-card fees and the house's cut gives adjusted gross. Eleven royalty participants take their share of that—pooled or at fixed rates, against adjusted gross or net, with a running offset that applies only while the show is still recouping. Then the weekly nut. Then the general partner's management fee, and any flat overrides on top of it. What survives is operating profit, and only then does the waterfall run.

Two structures ship. Under recoup-first, investors are repaid in full before anyone shares profit—and in the week the show crosses its capitalization, the engine splits that week's operating profit at the crossing point and runs the post-recoup split on the remainder alone, rather than treating the whole week as one side of the line. Under share-from-dollar-one, profit sharing runs alongside recoupment from the first performance. Around both sit per-investor ROI, cash-on-cash multiples, an approximate IRR, breakeven occupancy, and a sensitivity grid that re-runs the entire model across a matrix of occupancy rates and run lengths.

## Two seats

The producer—the general partner—needs control and auditability: every output should trace to an input they set. The prospective investor needs the opposite surface. Assumptions they can follow, outcomes they can compare, no account to create, and candour about the odds: the deal room's own disclosures tell a backer that only 20–25% of Broadway musicals fully recoup. The shipped dashboard takes both seats literally, its primary control a My Productions / My Investments toggle that filters to productions flagged as a personal investment. Both sides of the table, in one product, in the commit that first carried it.

## The decisions

These are trust decisions wearing engineering clothes: which surface is the source of truth, who gets to see a number, and what the software refuses to let drift. Each had a defensible opposite.

One rule earns a mention outside the ledger. The deal room shows aggregate capitalization and never individual investor records, so co-investors cannot see each other. It is asserted in eleven places and enforced in none—what actually makes it true is a single argument at a single call site, where the component hands over the stored form value rather than the bridged object holding every investor's name and amount. Both are in scope, three lines apart. It holds in production, where the only active deal room's investor array is empty.

## Checking the money

The engine was calibrated against a real show. Its documentation carries a check built from Hadestown's published economics—947 seats, 93% occupancy, a $155 average ticket, a $530K weekly nut, 14% royalties—together with what the model should return. I re-ran it against the shipped engine while writing this page, six months after the calibration was written and with nothing in CI holding it in place. It reproduces exactly: $1,046,283 weekly gross, 57.0% breakeven, recoupment in week 37.

That calibration, not the test suite, is what stands behind the arithmetic. Eighty-six tests pass, but only eight touch the engine and every one enters through the same file—the weekly pipeline, the phase function and the ownership rollup have no direct test at all. Two assertions check money against the inputs, both of them pool percentages; the ones that look like proof check the engine against its own output.

The separation that makes the money legible is structural. The engine is five files with no React and no Firebase import in any of them, so the weekly pipeline and the waterfall allocation exist in exactly one place and the investor's deal room computes its numbers by running the producer's own function—though display totals and percentages are re-derived in the UI in at least six places, one component recomputing by hand a result it already holds. A producer configures rates, splits, the waterfall type, scenario parameters, and which sections an investor sees. The engine fixes the order of the weekly deductions, the GP's carve coming out of the investor pool rather than the creative pool, and the cap table bridged in at computation time so the deal form cannot hold stale investor data. Nothing polices that division—no lint rule, no import restriction, no CI check. It holds because the rules files say so and reviewers read them.

![A rule the engine fixes rather than the producer, per hundred dollars of post-recoup profit: the GP's carve comes out of the investor pool, not the creative pool, so the limited partners absorb it and the creative share is untouched. The callout is the model declining to flatter the deal—this demonstration never recoups, so the split describes a structure that never pays out.](/images/projects/override-profit-split.png)

All of it arrived in a single commit titled "Updates via Claude Code," six days after the scaffold, and the product window closed six weeks later. Just six commits in the repository's history have ever touched the source; everything since is review and dependency apparatus. One gap in that apparatus is worth naming, because it is the kind agents do not catch: the repo's rules call the three engine files its highest-risk zone, while the automated review gate watches auth and payments directories that do not exist here. The judgment lives in prose and the machine gate aims elsewhere.

## The deal it has not run

Override has never run a real deal, and the scope of that claim is the repository, its commit history, and all 138 issues and pull requests down to every review comment: no real capitalization managed in it, no outside investor admitted, no deal-room link sent to a backer. The only deal room in production is a demonstration built with sample figures, and the closest thing to adoption in the record is me—an issue about slow dashboard loads names five productions in my own account. One instrument would sharpen the boundary: every deal-room load fires an analytics event keyed to the token, and I have never queried that log.

What the roadmap has already absorbed is worth separating from what it still lists. The requirements doc describes a wedge—financial modeling as the entry point, expanding outward from there—and part of that expansion shipped: the cap table, investor statuses and document storage are all in the product. The rest is written down as planned and not yet implemented: lead CRM, a signing workflow, investor reporting, payments and distributions, multi-production portfolio views. The code agrees on where the edge sits. `saveScenario()`, `getScenarios()` and `deleteScenario()` sit in the data layer with a subcollection and a security rule provisioned for them, and zero call sites anywhere in the application.

The bet was never that Broadway deserves bespoke software. It is that the drifted cap table had a cause—a producer and a backer each holding a version of the deal, with no way to know it was the same one—and that the fix for that is structural rather than editorial. One model, one place the money's order is defined, and a link you can send a backer that shows them what you are actually looking at. That is what Override is for, and a first real deal is what would prove it.
