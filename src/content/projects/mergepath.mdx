## The operator was the review process

Mergepath exists because one person was operating three agent platforms—Claude Code, Codex, Cursor—across a growing fleet of repositories, and the one rule that mattered was being followed only when convenient. Every change is required to go through a pull request: the rule was written into the instruction files the agents read. One of them would push straight to `main` anyway, usually on a change small enough not to feel like it counted.

Every time, I became the review process—reading diffs after the fact, relaying feedback between sessions, vetting uninspected output by hand. The agents produced more; my confidence did not keep up. That gap is the product problem.

The origin story is told in [Agent Approval Workflow and the Genesis of Mergepath](/blog/agent-approval-workflow-genesis-of-mergepath/). This page is about the decisions that came after the problem had a name, and what each has cost since.

## The authorities are deliberately separate

Six roles, and the whole design is in which of them may do what. The **operator** owns every repository and every setting the automation cannot reach, and is where escalation terminates. An **authoring agent** writes the change but opens it under one shared **author identity**, so what lands carries a single name rather than an agent's. Two gates stand between that pull request and the merge, and the reviewer agent behind the second one holds its own account precisely so that approving and authoring cannot be the same hand. The CI service account that runs the checks may never hold an approval at all, because GitHub counts an approval from any account with write access and offers nothing to tell a robot from a person. The **human tiebreaker** is the operator wearing a narrower hat, and it is a working role rather than a courtesy: the triggers that summon it are mechanical, so a finding re-flagged after a rebuttal, or a round budget spent on findings that keep blocking, escalates on its own. Nobody has to decide whether a disagreement is worth raising.

## The decisions

The easy description of Mergepath is an inventory—seven fail-closed CI checks in April 2026, twenty-seven by mid-May, seventy-two as of 2026-08-28, seventy-one of them wired into the lint workflow across eighty invocations—and the inventory is the least useful description, because a count says nothing about judgment. Most of the mechanisms in it exist because a specific failure happened while the fleet was live, and one principle runs under most of them: clearance is bound to the exact commit reviewed, and a content-changing push voids it. What follows are the five decisions a reasonable person could have made differently, and what each cost.

## What the record shows, and what it does not

Adoption is measurable. Impact is not, and holding those apart is most of what this section can honestly do.

<ConstraintStrip constraints={props.constraints} />

The fleet got to eight repositories by shrinking: one consumer was archived in August 2026 and retired from the manifest, and another was dropped in September after it went dormant. The stress test came in July 2026: 134 review-finding threads across a 24-hour, eleven-PR record, dissected in [A Perfect Score on the Wrong Axis](/blog/perfect-score-wrong-axis/). And the sharpest result cuts against the hub rather than for it. Propagation dropped files the template's own review had already cleared into two unfamiliar codebases, and seven external-review rounds per pull request went on finding bugs in them. Review found the least where the files were most familiar.

Propagation reports its own state. A read-only audit on 2026-08-28 reported the same eighteen manifest paths adrift on all eight consumers then enrolled—and the identical set is the tell: every consumer had been synced to the hub's `main` that afternoon, and the eighteen paths were the previous evening's merges that the operator's local hub checkout had not yet pulled. A directory entry such as `scripts/ci/` counts as one path however many files inside it moved:

```text
$ scripts/sync-to-downstream.sh --audit
nathanpaynedotcom (nathanjohnpayne/nathanpaynedotcom)
  baseline: main@eafa46b
  ✗ .github/workflows/agent-review.yml         drift: 1751 diff line(s)
  ✗ tests/test_approval_merge_continuation.sh  drift: 996 diff line(s)
  ✗ scripts/merge-clearance-gate.sh            drift: 78 diff line(s)
  ✗ scripts/ci/                                drift: 3 file(s) drifted
  … 14 more paths, and the same eighteen on all seven other consumers
```

That is still the mechanism working rather than failing: drift is a reviewable diff instead of a weekly discovery, whichever side of the comparison has moved. It is a snapshot, not a steady state—a bulk sync closes the gap, and several in this repository's history have. What this one shows is a stale checkout, not a fleet between waves.

What none of it measures is what the gates save. No manual baseline was ever recorded, so there is nothing to subtract from; the one savings figure the repository computes multiplies auto-approved pull requests by a hardcoded thirty-to-180-minute constant that nobody measured. The two real measurement systems both measure cost—how long external review takes, and the latency budget the CI suite has to stay inside.

## The operator gets a console

Until October 2026 the operator's own picture of the fleet was assembled by hand: `gh pr checks` for the merge gates, `gh run view` for the failing step, the Phase 4b ledgers for what the review agents had spent, and the sync audit above for drift—one command at a time, per repository. Mergepath Cockpit puts that picture on one live page. It is a local server, Python standard library with no third-party packages, shelling out to the hub's installed `yq`, `jq`, `gh` and `git`, bound to the loopback address, polling GitHub every fifteen seconds to two minutes and pushing what changes to the page over server-sent events. The resident server holds the cached reviewer token only—the author token is stripped from its environment—so the console can watch the fleet and cannot merge into it. Its one write is a fleet sync, and that runs as a preview the operator reads and then confirms.

![Mergepath Cockpit on 2026-10-06, captured from the operator's local session during a refinement pass, on live data. The dashboard is held to the same discipline as the gates. Every panel starts unavailable rather than clear, a failed read keeps the last good observation and shows its age, and partial coverage is never reported as a clear fleet. One gap is on the record: the account billing endpoint answered 404 to the reviewer credential, so Actions spend reads unavailable rather than zero, and nobody has established whether the cause is the token or the endpoint. The epic opened on 2026-10-02 and closed on 2026-10-04, which means nobody has run the fleet through this console long enough to say what it changes.

## The premise the fleet did not meet

Then the adverse finding, which is the one I would want read. A GitHub job gates a merge only when branch protection lists it as a required check. Absent that listing it still runs, still goes red, and the pull request merges anyway. On 2026-07-28 the fleet's own audit found eight of the ten repositories then enrolled enforcing zero of five canonical checks, three with no protection on `main` at all, and the hub itself enforcing two. That is a dated snapshot, and the gap has since closed: the five checks have been required on every repository the weekly audit can read since at least 2026-08-19, and the one finding it still reports is a single consumer's admin bypass under the 2026-09-06 staged decision.

Which meant that until that audit, red CI could look like enforcement while being advisory—on a standard whose entire premise is that enforcement must happen at a boundary that can refuse.

## What it bought the other projects

Five of the seven projects on this site are consumers of this standard; a sixth is Mergepath itself, and the seventh left the fleet in September 2026 when its repository went dormant. What the standard bought them is that their pages get to be about their products: features shipped mid-sailing on Five Across landed as reviewed pull requests, and Override—whose engine was built a month before any of this existed—has run every change to its product code since on the fleet's review path, while thirty governance, docs and sync commits went straight to its `main`, the last on 2026-08-28. Those pages spend a line on governance and the rest on the product. This page is where that line gets audited.

One disclosure belongs at the end, because it is this page's own validation boundary: the pull request that ships it will merge through the system it describes. That proves the choreography runs. It proves nothing else—a system approving its own description is exactly the evidence this standard was built to distrust.
