## The answer depended on who you asked

Can this device support Dolby Vision? Which partners are still running an ADK build that just went end-of-life? When a partner replaces its whole OS stack—Amazon moving Fire TV to Vega OS—which devices are affected, and what is their certification status? Inside Disney Streaming those questions arrive constantly, from partner engineering, certification, support, and platform teams, and every one of them is answerable. The trouble was where the answers lived.

They lived in Airtable, in Datadog, in partner-submitted Excel questionnaires, and in spreadsheets maintained by different teams on different cadences. What the fragmentation cost is my testimony rather than the repository's: support tickets that should have been lookups, launch timelines that slipped during reconciliation, and partner conversations held without a shared factual foundation.

Device Source of Truth is the internal Disney Streaming answer: one system that imports from every source, normalizes against a shared schema, and turns loose operational memory into a queryable, auditable record. This is the partner-engineering work I spent a decade doing at Disney, and DST is the tool I wanted to exist before I sat down to build it. **What is on this page is a fork of that system, running on data I invented for it.** Not a reimplementation written from memory, and not the production instance either: the same codebase, with the real records replaced. It does not model those four teams as personas; it enforces three roles—a viewer reads everything, an editor authors device and partner records directly and stages the imports, an admin alone commits an import or deletes anything—and forty of the API's fifty role guards are admin-only, the codebase's clearest statement of where the risk lives.

## Five feeds, one gate

Five feeds land in the registry: an AllModels CSV of the device inventory, Airtable intake requests, partner-key mappings exported from Datadog, telemetry snapshots from Datadog, and the partner's Excel questionnaire—a workbook commonly carrying 100–150 answered questions across sixteen sections, mapped onto a 260-field device model, with AI extraction reading it in 30-pair batches. Extraction shipped fire-and-forget once and was rebuilt as an idempotent per-device task queue with retry and stale-job recovery.

The feeds share one shape, and the shape is the judgment: every path is a two-step preview-then-commit, and every commit requires the admin role. Nothing in the interface writes an imported record without a human having reviewed a preview of it. For four of the five feeds that is a workflow rather than a server-side invariant—the commit routes check the admin role and then import the rows they are handed, so an admin calling the API directly is not made to look at anything first. The questionnaire path is the exception, and it is why that one carries the page's strongest fact: it stages to its own collections and the approve route refuses until every staged record has been reviewed. No single screen announces the rule; it is only visible across the import surface as a whole—hence a diagram rather than a screenshot.

What the registry cannot explain becomes a work item. A telemetry row naming a device the registry does not hold, or a partner key it does not know, raises an alert carrying the control that creates the missing record. The two halves close differently: creating a partner key auto-dismisses every matching alert server-side, while an unregistered device is registered and its alert then dismissed by hand. Two alert types are in service, both raised by telemetry uploads; a third is declared, labelled, and filterable, and nothing in the audited code writes it. The other feeds raise no alerts—they surface their exceptions inline, in their own previews.

## The decisions

The AI trust boundary is where this product argues with the defaults, and the same codebase draws the line twice, differently. On the CSV disambiguation paths, a name the model resolves at 0.90 confidence stops asking its clarification question—though it still rides the preview-then-commit rail. On the questionnaire path, the model reports a confidence per extracted field and the number decides nothing: it colors the review screen, and review is unconditional. Extraction was allowed to be the labor and never the authority. The records below price that position and the others like it.

## Nothing real is on display

Everything in those screenshots is invented. Forking a working internal system is the cheap half; making it safe to show is the rest, and the seam is visible in what the scrub reached and what it did not. The deployed instance has run entirely on synthetic seed data since an August 20, 2026 scrub replaced the real records: fourteen fictional operators—Northwind Cable, Brightloom Telecom, Quillon TVs—under a fictional streaming group named Story Entertainment, every seeded document carrying a synthetic marker. The dataset's header opens "Every name in this file is invented"—no real partners, operators, OEMs, chipsets, or device models, and nothing derived from a real partner submission. Where production said ADK, the demo says SEK, the invented "Story Entertainment Kit"—a display-layer rename, not a data change: the schema field keeps its name, and "only the values users actually see change." SEK on a screenshot is itself the tell that the data is fake.

The boundary runs deeper than the demo, and it is why this page's evidence is screenshots. The repository is private and stays private: its specs and history still carry real partner identities that a scrub of deployed data does not reach. So this page offers no repository link at all, rather than pointing a reader at a 404 only its owner can pass; the one button it does carry stops at a domain-restricted login. The captures above are the inspectable evidence: real workflows running on invented data.

## What the record shows, and what it does not

The workflows shipped and the deployed instance runs them end to end, but no artifact records a team adopting the tool or a production question answered with it. Development stopped on 6 March 2026, before any adoption period, and the usage events the application emits were never reported against—so the claim this page can stand behind is that the design decisions were made and built, not that they were validated in use.

The instrumentation shipped and the measurements did not. The application declares an analytics union of more than forty events—device search, questionnaire upload, alert dismissal, and more—wired to Firebase Analytics, and nothing in the repository reports what any of them recorded. Since the scrub, whatever they record is demo traffic.

The commit record since March 6 has a precise shape: dependency bumps, template synchronization, and CI work, plus the synthetic-data scrub and three rounds of security hardening through August 2026—input bounds, then CORS scoping and rate limiting, then volumetric limits. Not adoption evidence—but the one outcome-shaped fact here: an archived tool still hardened rather than left to rot.

How the work was run is in the record; how much of it agents wrote is not—agents commit under the human author identity, so bylines are uninformative, and I will not pretend otherwise. What the record does hold is the governance: sixteen agent-facing rule documents plus per-agent configuration for three platforms; the shared [Mergepath](/projects/mergepath/) review pipeline, which arrived finished by template propagation eighteen days after the last product feature and so governs the maintenance tail, not the build; CodeRabbit carrying device-domain review guidance; and a two-strike rule that forces any third attempt at the same bug to open with a written audit of the first two.

## What it demonstrates

With no adoption period, the demonstration is the decisions themselves: a human signature made non-negotiable exactly where a wrong commit would poison the registry, and machine resolution permitted where a wrong guess costs one question; a registry that refuses to let older data silently replace newer; age shown to the reader and never enforced on them; identity resolved rather than scrubbed. All of it is enforced in code, behind a login a reader cannot pass—so the page shows the enforcement the only way it can: running, on data invented to be shown.
