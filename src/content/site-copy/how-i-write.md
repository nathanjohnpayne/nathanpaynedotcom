AI agents write the posts and case studies on this site. An agent, usually Claude, writes the first draft from my direction, my own account of what happened, and the record of the work: commits, pull requests, issues, and session transcripts. Agents, mostly Claude and Codex, revise it from there, and AI reviewers flag problems for them to fix.

One of those revisions is a pass for voice. It works from my notes and from passages I chose as style examples. So far those examples are agent revisions I approved, not writing I did on my own.

Agents also check the facts. Every post and case study has a facts ledger in the site's repository: an agent lists the piece's claims, checks each one against the record, and marks it supported, wrong, stale, or unprovable. A ledger covers the text as it stood when it was checked, and some claims rest on my own account, which no record can confirm.

My part is direction and judgment. I choose what each piece covers, supply what I saw and decided, and send drafts back with notes. I have the final say on what a piece argues and how it sounds, and the opinions in it are mine: the workflow forbids agents from inventing my involvement, motives, or judgments. I read every piece in full and approve it before it's published.

Each post and case study ends with a statement headed "How this was written." It says what agents and I did on that piece, and it's the specific account; this page describes the general practice. The full process is public: <a href="https://github.com/nathanjohnpayne/nathanpaynedotcom/blob/56bb6610e23c2f355fb658df670b7b1a0e72fd41/docs/blog-writing-workflow.md" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Blog drafting and voice review</a>.

This page covers the posts and case studies. Other copy on the site, such as the homepage and the résumé, carries no statement of its own; agents write and edit that copy too. It doesn't cover writing I publish elsewhere, or the software itself; the case studies say how each project was built.
