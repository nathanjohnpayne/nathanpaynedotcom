## Most changed passages

### The old site

**Before**

The site was seven `index.html` files and one global `style.css`: a homepage and four project case studies I maintained by hand, plus a blog index and one post rendered by a 677-line Markdown generator I had written myself, every page carrying its own pasted copy of the chrome. I built it the way a product manager builds things: copy an HTML file, edit what changes, ship it. I am not an engineer, and the chassis shows both halves of that: I shipped something real without stopping to learn a framework, and I did not notice for weeks that what I had shipped could not grow.

**After**

The site had seven `index.html` files and one global `style.css`. I maintained the homepage and four project case studies by hand. A 677-line Markdown generator I'd written produced the blog index and its one post. Every page carried a pasted copy of the chrome.

I built it as a product manager, not an engineer: copy an HTML file, change what I needed, and ship it. That got a real site up without first learning a framework. It took weeks to notice what adding to it would cost.

### The publishing problem

**Before**

The patch worked. It did not satisfy me. Adding a post was supposed to mean writing Markdown and running `node scripts/generate-blog.js`, which rendered the post and the index. By that morning it could not: the committed HTML carried hand-edits the generator could not produce, and at 10:47am, forty-three minutes after the fix merged, I [filed the ticket](https://github.com/nathanjohnpayne/nathanpaynedotcom/issues/33) cataloguing ten ways its output had drifted. A second post meant repairing a 677-line hand-rolled Markdown parser or hand-patching its output again, forever. The visible symptom was "this page overflows on mobile." The structural reality was five pages of hand-edited chrome, two more generated by a parser I had already hand-patched around, and no way to add a post without touching all of it.

**After**

The patch worked. Adding a post still meant writing Markdown and running `node scripts/generate-blog.js`, except the generator couldn't reproduce the hand-edits in the committed post and index. At 10:47am, forty-three minutes after the fix merged, I [filed a ticket](https://github.com/nathanjohnpayne/nathanpaynedotcom/issues/33) listing ten ways its output had drifted. A second post meant fixing the 677-line parser or hand-patching its output again.

Five pages of hand-edited chrome, two more generated by a parser I had already hand-patched around, and no way to add a post without touching all of it.

### Starting that afternoon

**Before**

That timing was not an accident, and the record keeps the hours. The fix merged at 10:04am. The generator-drift ticket went in at 10:47am. At 12:46pm a static site generator evaluation report landed on it, and at 1:13pm an eleven-phase migration plan. The whole architecture was loaded in my head, sins included, and the cost of starting the migration would never be lower. Close the laptop, and tomorrow that context is gone.

**After**

That timing wasn't an accident. The fix merged at 10:04am, the generator-drift ticket went in at 10:47am, the evaluation report arrived at 12:46pm, and the eleven-phase plan at 1:13pm. I had the whole architecture in my head, sins included. Close the laptop, and tomorrow that context is gone.

### The missing cutover record

**Before**

That leaves a hole in the timeline: **the moment the site actually went live on Astro is not in the repository's record.** It plainly happened—the site runs on Astro today and every later phase of work builds on the Astro tree—but the deploy was a manual command with no workflow behind it, and no smoke-test result or ticked checklist dates it; only Firebase's own release history could. A migration case study that cannot evidence its own cutover is awkward to write, but the alternative is letting an issue-closure timestamp quietly stand in for a deployment.

**After**

**The repository doesn't date when the site went live on Astro.** It happened—the site runs on Astro, and later work uses that tree—but deployment was a manual command without a workflow. There's no smoke-test result or completed checklist to date it. Firebase's own release history could. An issue closing is a poor substitute for a cutover timestamp, even in a migration I'd like to call a one-day success.

### Cost and estimate

**Before**

The estimate: had I done this by hand—learned Astro well enough to ship cleanly, ported the homepage, blog, and project pages, debugged the OG integration, written the Playwright suite, rewired the Firebase deploy, and propagated the documentation—my honest guess is somewhere between a long weekend and a vacation week of focused engineering time. That is an estimate of a migration that never ran; no manual baseline exists. But the decision was made against that estimate, and I do not have those weeks.

**After**

Doing it by hand would have meant learning enough Astro, porting the homepage, blog, and project pages, debugging OG generation, writing the Playwright suite, changing the Firebase deploy, and updating the documentation. My honest guess for doing this by hand was somewhere between a long weekend and a vacation week. I don't have those weeks.

That's an estimate of work I never did, with no manual baseline. It was still the estimate I made the decision against.

### Ending

**Before**

The hard part is not noticing the ceiling. It is that the smaller, faster, cheaper-looking option is already merged by mid-morning, and the structural option asks you to keep going anyway. The record shows the choice that got made: [PR #30](https://github.com/nathanjohnpayne/nathanpaynedotcom/pull/30) and [PR #47](https://github.com/nathanjohnpayne/nathanpaynedotcom/pull/47) sit four hours apart in the git log, with the phase issues for everything in between opened before the scaffold had even merged.


**After**

The smaller option was already merged by mid-morning. I had to decide whether to keep going. I chose the build chain and its maintenance costs because I wanted to keep publishing. The patch made the post readable; the migration made another post practical.
